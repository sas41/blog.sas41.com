# Preface
 It's been a rough few days for the game, Bulkhead and the players.  On one hand, a ton of criticism leveled at Bulkhead and on the other, Joe Brammer's reaction to some of it has driven a wedge between what I feel are, a very enthusiastic community and a very communicative group of developers.

So, to Joe Brammer, I have included a section at the end, if this somehow reaches your eyes, please read that last section.


# Foreword
 From the very beginning of Wardogs' marketing, there have been signs of worry and insecurity about the project as a whole so I think it's important to outline a few examples first:

 - Warning players in [official marketing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ugkuP4a3xk4) ^[article](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/fps/hardcore-fps-immediately-becomes-steam-top-seller-as-devs-list-10-reasons-not-to-buy-it/)^ ^[archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20261117202908/https://www.pcgamer.com/games/fps/hardcore-fps-immediately-becomes-steam-top-seller-as-devs-list-10-reasons-not-to-buy-it/)^ material about negative reviews and online sentiment before the game is even out.

 - Repeatedly telling the community that they know what they are doing and know what they want to make.
 
 - Saying "Players are good at identifying problems but not solutions" (paraphrased) and then supposedly taking advice from streamers (people who play games for a job).

 - Constantly, publicly asking for reviews.

There are more examples that I can think of but it's hard to find sources when most of their communication is in video form and [Filmot](https://filmot.com/) can only help so much.

# The Game

## The progression / unlock system
 There are a few contradictions in Wardogs' design. Bulkhead refers to the game as a Sandbox FPS, which it very much does feel like in many ways, that part is accurate, but what contradicts with that design philosophy is the current progression system. It's known by now that they plan to wholesale replace this progression system very soon with something that sounds much nicer (from what I understand, the Intel system is like tokens you can spend on unlocking what you're interested in specifically instead of climbing different vertical ladders).
 
 However, what I am struggling to reconcile is why there has been so much time and effort put into the vertical progression system that is on its way out. As of writing, Season 3 just came out, and was the trigger point that led to this blog post. In Season 3 they reworked the progression system quite dramatically both in its numbers and how unlocks are dispersed.

 On one hand, having to unlock things is at odds with the sandbox elements of the core design, and on the other, such systems are clearly valuable enough for retention that Bulkhead decided to spend time and resources adding it. I don't think it's a bad idea, having something to work towards is fun, but as it stands, this progression and unlock system causes three major headaches I want to point out below, starting with...

## Balance
 So much has been said and suggested, so I want to keep my point short and concise. In a game where the balancing act is akin to playing Rock, Paper and Scissors, it's brutally punishing to not have access to Paper when someone is caving your face in with a Rock. No need to talk about if Paper is good where it is now, those discussions will never end, but what's important is that the tools to deal with problems must exist for the game to be interesting and not frustrating, and very importantly, the effectiveness to skill requirement ratio for both the problem and the solution must be roughly in the same place.

 Telling people to just "shoot at the littlebird" or "just get an RPG" is not going to earn anyone favors, when shooting at them feels pointless this season and the RPG itself is a genuine burden and major loadout decision to carry. It's contradictory to the "Play your way" mantra to be forced to carry a Verba or RPG with me at all times because the sky is full of Minigun LBs like mosquitos in a swamp.

## Time Investment
 I should say, I am biased on this front, but I think my point of view is the healthier one when it comes to games. I think games are good in moderation, and games that lend themselves well to healthy amounts of time investment and fair monetization are obviously superior and better for society overall. While people who play more are likelier to have noticed more problems, they are also more skewed to see some things as problematic that, for the vast majority of people, are not problematic at all.

 That is to say, the best thing a game can do for it's players is respect their time investment. Games with seasonal wipes tend to offer a unique experience for each wipe. The reason why it worked for Tarkov and continues to do so is not just the "Early Wipe" experience, it's also all the new content that entices people to come back. That's why even after launch Tarkov is trying to mix things up with its "Seasonal" mode.

 It's hard to say so early if wipes are good for Wardogs or not, simply because we have yet to have a good baseline for what a Season should be.
Season 1 was okay; it outlined some problems, but it was too short for most people to get a good sense of the game's wider sandbox.
Season 2 was off to a bad start in terms of perception, because the second problem with unlocks after pacing was the verticality of it, people who went past the level 20 hump for a class were met with Class 3 Armor + AP ammo or Class 4 Helmet snipers, and builders, making the game, once again, frustrating to play.

 Season 3, the current season as of writing, is off to a bad start because of the XP requirement changes (and the Minigun LB), people struggled to get level 20 unlocks in Season 1's ~6-week timeframe, let alone the signature level 35 unlocks, and now the XP requirement for those unlocks has more than doubled when the wipe length itself has, at best, doubled.

## The Mismatch between what the game is and what it should be
 This last point is not self-evident so I will try my best to explain: Developers will often have a vision for their game, but sometimes the game they end up making isn't quite what they intended to make. If a game with such a mismatch sells very well, this can create a very troublesome situation that can become very toxic, very quickly.

 My go-to example nowadays is Arrowhead and Helldivers 2. Arrowhead says they are making a grunt-fantasy, boots-on-the ground brutal game where you're supposed to beat the odds. Meanwhile, their game has been a power fantasy since launch, and people have kept that power fantasy alive despite Arrowhead's repeated efforts to curb it. The final result is a developer that seemingly hates its player base and a player base that has no faith in the developer, developing the game. It's difficult to find good things to say about Arrowhead.

 So how does this relate to Wardogs? Well, it's the thing about Sandbox vs Progression thing, how is Bulkhead ever supposed to balance things when:
  - A) The progression is vertical, so investing time into unlocking something has to be worth it.
  - B) The people who DO get those things will often be a self-selected few who played far more than the average player with the selection pool only shrinking as XP requirements grow.

  This, to me, is a fundamentally impossible problem to "solve"; the best that can be done is compromise, and compromises, unfortunately, often lead to "pissing everyone off" as opposed to "making everyone happy".

# My Feedback: The Game

If Season 4 will have the Intel system, I think there is little point in me trying to address these problems constructively. But if I were Bulkhead, I would have made Season 3 have no unlock system at all, just the full sandbox available from the get-go. I would make every weapon and vehicle have a place in the sandbox and would try my best to balance things with very small changes very often, like weekly balance patches and minor bugfixes.

This way we can finally get a good sense of what the game is supposed to be, and in the future we can give much better constructive feedback when something feels under or overpowered in the sandbox.

# The Studio
 Just going to preface this section with the following: I do not care that Battalion 1944 didn't work out so well, I don't think it should haunt Bulkhead and honestly, seeing their philosophy with that game and Wardogs, I am at least assured that their views on gaming and mine are aligned.

## The Pendulum
 However, I think the balancing team does deserve some criticism, because despite decades of game design lessons learned and shared, they fell victim to a classic problem: Pendulum Balancing.

 You might have seen this in other games, it happens quite often, mostly on a small scale usually the oscillating stops as gradually something is reined in. A perfect example of this is Counter-Strike, since 1.5 the game has had different balancing eras, but it has overall been a very solid series when it comes to balance. New games tend to come very seldom and shake things up, the balance then settles into a meta, and even when something clearly broken gets into the game, the oscillation stops relatively quickly (my god that R8 on release was something to behold). CS2 today has problems for sure, but it's also a game where there is a clear vision for what the balancing should achieve and it's very conservative with it, so what can Wardogs borrow when it's likely to be a lot more liberal with its additions to its sandbox? The answer is above, tiny, incremental changes as opposed to waiting weeks to make big changes and have the pendulum swing the other way and make everyone sit through something patently unfun the whole time.

## The Communication
 Bulkhead are very communicative with the community and I think that's great, I appreciate it for sure but more than that, it's good to see people who make games, enjoy making them. However, being in the spotlight has its downsides, especially when your audience is this big, and this varied, and your game is in a genre that tends to attract toxicity, this can quickly lead to a state of decay and erosion.
 
 Joe Brammer has positioned themselves as the public face of the company, and has repeatedly asked that the community spare their development team and level criticism at them and leave the staff alone. This doesn't look like a new thing for them either, I managed to find [sources for this kind of thinking from as early as 2019](https://www.pcgamesn.com/battalion-1944/development-updates-dlc) ^[archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20261117201734/https://www.pcgamesn.com/battalion-1944/development-updates-dlc)^. This is noble and kind of them but honestly, that can't be good, given that their marketing has already invited and braced itself for all kinds of criticism.

 This has seemingly led to a somewhat predictable situation where [a video criticising Bulkhead by EclipseFPS](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9z7MquYHUmE) caught Joe's attention and [they reacted to it live on twitch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cc1q4u65Jqc).

 In their reaction to the video, Joe's usual speak-my-mind attitude led to them saying some concerning things, despite both Joe and Eclipse being seemingly cool with each other's video/reaction the community latched onto a few things, and quite frankly, they were worth being concerned about.

 Phrases such as "If you don't like it, go make your own game" or "we don't talk about the gold market" are generally not what people want to hear when the perception about the balance and the gold market are at an all-time low. That's not public relations management; that's pouring gasoline into the fire, and it runs counter of what Joe claims they want to do, take the flak away from the rest of the team.

 And I think it's important to talk about tone here too, because Joe's right about one thing, Reddit can be a cesspool, because as a platform it provides all the wrong incentives, a single downvote can easily bury positivity and constructive feedback and content can often be gate-kept by people who are terminally on the platform as opposed to a healthy mix of the community. But the tone of a place like that is also set by you, your statements and actions. 
 
 To that end, I still constantly see the "10 reasons you shouldn't buy Wardogs" style of replies on there, toxic troglodytes telling others "maybe the game is not for you" pretty much every chance they get, and hearing that from the face of the studio is not really going to help. Valid criticism can often be shut down long before anyone close to average has a chance to see it on there and Twitter is not a real pressure gauge either, given its algorithmic nature and how skewed and manipulated it is.
 
# My Feedback: The Studio
 I hope someday I can carve some time out and work on an independent platform for constructive feedback like [Tarkov.Community](https://tarkov.community/) for all games. But until I or someone else makes something like this, the best Bulkhead can do is survey owners of the game via Steam login or in-game. It will be much healthier for everyone and make sentiment analysis more accurate.

 I also think Joe Brammer engaging with criticism directly is bad for everyone involved, I think it's healthier to engage with criticism in general, I suggest having a person whose job it is to collect criticism and relay that to other parties in a sanitized way, someone to help filter the frustration that can often accompany criticism. (I know they said they use AI to collect and distill such information but as someone in the field I honestly would not trust it with that.)

 
 # To Joe

 It can't be easy to be in this position. It would be all too easy for people to say that you made your own bed and that you should lie in it, but I think that's unfair. Taking the heat from everyone is noble but unhealthy. You deserve better and I am sure you will be much better off taking a few steps back. You made a game people are passionate about, and care enough to talk about. This is already different to Battalion 1944, if in no other way than the scale of it. You already made something people want, something people care about. Take care of yourself the way you care for your staff.
